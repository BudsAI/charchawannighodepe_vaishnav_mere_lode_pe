About me

I’m a third-year Ph.D. candidate in the Data Analytics concentration at ESSEC Business School, supervised by Roberto Renò. I study what short-dated options reveal about intraday volatility and how options trading relates to household borrowing.

Before ESSEC, I worked as a research associate at CAFRAL, Reserve Bank of India, and as a research assistant at the Centre for Data Science and Artificial Intelligence at IIM Ahmedabad. I hold a BS–MS in Mathematics from IISER Kolkata.

Selected research

Working papers

0DTE options · Intraday volatility

Too Short to Ignore: Forecasting Intraday Volatility with 0DTE Options

With Fulvio Corsi (University of Pisa), Nicola Fusari (Johns Hopkins Carey Business School), and Roberto Renò (ESSEC Business School).

Read abstract

We forecast intraday volatility using high-frequency S&P 500 option and price data, assessing the incremental predictive content of the implied volatility from short-dated options. We develop an interval-specific forecasting framework that aligns volatility measurement and prediction horizons at the intraday level. We show that implied volatility extracted from same-day expiry options (0DTE options) contains statistically and economically meaningful predictive information beyond that of standard regressors. Including 0DTE implied volatility reduces out-of-sample forecast MSE by 19% on average, with particularly pronounced gains at the open and close of the trading session and in the period after FOMC announcements.

Retail options · Household credit

Real Effect of Speculation: Option Market to Credit Market

With Pulak Ghosh (IIM Bangalore), Preetesh Kantak (Indiana University Kelley School of Business), and Varun Sharma (Indiana University Kelley School of Business).

Read abstract

Is the global surge in retail options trading debt-driven? Combining India’s countrywide administrative credit registry with near-universe trades from one of the world’s largest options exchanges, we show that option trading and consumer borrowing move together: areas with higher participation in a quarter see more credit applications and larger loan balances. For identification, we exploit a regulatory change that raised the minimum tradable quantity of options. The higher participation cost drove some households out, but those who remained doubled their outlay to meet the new threshold. Areas more exposed to the change saw faster consumer credit growth. The additional borrowing came through credit cards, overdrafts, gold loans, and consumer loans from FinTech and non-bank lenders. Mortgages and vehicle loans did not respond, as they would if improving local economic conditions were driving credit growth. Individual accounts at a large bank and its broker tell the same story: customers borrow more in the quarters in which they trade more, and the premium they pay for options rises after they take out a personal, consumer, or gold loan. Our results suggest that household options participation is partly debt-financed, and that regulations raising participation costs can unintentionally increase borrowing.

Other writing

Shrinkage Is All You Need: Evidence from BVARs with SPF Priors

This application tests whether priors drawn from the Survey of Professional Forecasters improve Bayesian VAR forecasts. The best-performing model tunes the shrinkage parameter and assigns zero weight to the survey forecasts.

CP and Tucker Tensor Decomposition of Bilateral Trade Data

Using bilateral trade data for 27 major economies from 2010 to 2024, this project compares CP and Tucker tensor decompositions. Four exporter–importer patterns and a common time trend provide a compact representation of the trade data.
